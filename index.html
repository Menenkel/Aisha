AISHA — AI Safety in Humanitarian Assistance

Stronger humanitarian action.Greater resilience in an AI-shaped world.

We work with humanitarian and resilience partners to assess where AI helps and where it fails, build safeguards and local capability for responsible use, and prepare for crises AI could cause or accelerate.

Why it matters

AI is already in the systems people depend on. Safe practice hasn’t caught up.

When those systems fail, the consequences reach beyond a single wrong answer. A forecast can remain confident after the evidence beneath it has changed. If organisations rely on the same model, dataset or provider, a failure that goes unnoticed can shape decisions across an entire response.

AI may also help create or accelerate the crises humanitarian systems must confront, through false information, cyberattacks or biological threats. Those systems will need to recognise what is happening and keep working under pressure, even as they rely on AI themselves.

Humanitarian work is one of the clearest places to build that capability: decisions are consequential, information is thin, and there is rarely time to check. Stronger human judgment, clear responsibility and systems that can withstand failure matter far beyond this sector.

What we do

AISHA brings together humanitarian, resilience and AI expertise to strengthen the systems people depend on. Our two complementary focus areas connect crisis preparedness with accountable, effective AI use.

Crisis resilience

Prevent and respond to catastrophic AI risks and systemic harm

AI can create new threats and undermine the global systems needed to manage crises. Drawing on our team’s extensive experience with the global humanitarian architecture and resilience systems, we can help partners identify risks, anticipate cascading impacts, strengthen early warning and coordinate preparedness and response across institutions and sectors. This includes external AI-enabled threats and failures or misuse of AI within these systems.

Explore crisis resilience capabilities

Illustrative activities we can support:

  • Analytics for early warning, anticipatory action and response

    Drawing on our team’s work with UN agencies—including WFP, FAO and OCHA—government ministries and humanitarian organisations, we help partners integrate AI-related catastrophic risks into essential warning and response systems. We map how these systems collect, analyse and act on information. For example, we can examine how manipulated data or overreliance on AI forecasts can delay famine warnings, and strengthen independent verification, expert judgment and coordinated early action.

  • Rumour tracking and AI-related information risk

    AI makes convincing misinformation easier to create, target and spread, posing urgent risks to public trust and crisis response. Drawing on our team’s rumour-tracking and risk-communication experience in humanitarian crises, including Ebola and COVID-19, we can help embed and scale established humanitarian practice across essential systems. This means adapting approaches to AI-enabled threats and building partnerships among local organisations, government agencies, universities and specialist technology partners to anticipate threats, detect harmful claims, verify evidence and coordinate trusted responses.

  • Urban resilience and catastrophic AI risk

    Dense populations, high vulnerability and dependence on interconnected services leave cities exposed to cascading AI-related crises. Disruptions to power, water, communications or trusted information can spread across sectors, with mass displacement compounding the harm.

    Our team brings extensive experience in urban humanitarian assistance, resilience and capacity building with local organisations, municipalities, ministries, national agencies, universities and businesses—including finance, construction and essential services. Drawing on this experience, we can help partners map dependencies, test disruption scenarios and integrate AI safeguards into workflows within and between institutions. This strengthens locally led prevention, preparedness and response through existing municipal, national and humanitarian systems.

    AISHA is developing its first urban pilot in the Guatemala City metropolitan area, connecting local knowledge and institutional capabilities to strengthen resilience to AI-related and other crises.

  • Crisis preparedness, assessment and response

    AI-associated crises require coordinated action across humanitarian and development systems. Our team brings extensive assessment and response experience across Africa, Asia, Latin America and the Caribbean, and the United States. We can help networks of agencies and local organisations strengthen their capacity to detect emerging threats, assess impacts and coordinate responses. Our systems approach builds on local leadership, existing relationships and shared responsibilities across institutions. We can support practical training, joint exercises and response planning, and provide experienced advisers to accompany partners remotely or on site during crises.

Resilient Systems

Strengthen accountable, effective and safe AI use in essential global systems for crisis prevention, response and recovery.

We can help government, humanitarian, civil society, university and private-sector partners strengthen evidence, staff expertise, safe workflows and infrastructure across connected systems. Our approach combines technical advising with shared governance to improve decisions and coordination while protecting independent judgment. The aim is stronger local authority over knowledge and action, less reliance on external actors, and reliable operations through AI-related disruptions and other crises.

Explore resilient systems capabilities

Illustrative activities we can support:

  • Institutional capacity and reliable AI infrastructure

    Drawing on our team’s experience strengthening local humanitarian and resilience institutions, we can help partners build the skills, infrastructure and support needed for reliable AI use. Our developing Capable Nodes approach assesses each institution’s responsibilities, workflows, computing, connectivity and training needs. We can help partners establish staged improvement plans, connect with technical and funding expertise, and maintain essential functions when tools fail. Practical coaching strengthens professional expertise and independent judgment alongside AI skills.

  • Locally governed knowledge ecosystems

    Effective humanitarian action depends on knowledge held across communities, public agencies, universities, businesses and humanitarian organisations. Building on our experience in knowledge management and strengthening local systems, we can help partners connect this evidence through locally governed knowledge ecosystems. Our approach combines shared information, professional networks and accountable AI use to support analysis, coordination and learning. Partners retain control over their contributions, can trace and challenge evidence, and build collective knowledge that endures beyond individual projects or external support.

  • Technical advising, assessments and learning

    Our team brings extensive experience in humanitarian assessment, resilience programming, analytics, and monitoring, evaluation, research and learning. We can help partners apply AI to practical decisions—from identifying vulnerable populations and assessing needs to comparing programme options and learning from results. Our technical advising connects sector expertise, local evidence and appropriate analytical methods. We can help teams verify findings in the field, identify uncertainty and test whether AI improves the quality and usefulness of their work while strengthening staff capability and institutional memory.

  • Information governance and safe workflows

    Drawing on our team’s experience managing knowledge and working with sensitive humanitarian data, we can help institutions govern how information enters, moves through and informs their systems. With partners, we review real workflows, establish which information may enter which AI tools, and assign responsibility for checking outputs and acting on them. This includes protecting confidential information, keeping AI-generated material distinguishable from source evidence, and establishing correction and fallback procedures. The aim is trustworthy shared information and accountable decisions within and across institutions.

Supporting both focus areas

Safe AI Innovation Lab

Our Safe AI Innovation Lab brings together data scientists, humanitarian and disaster risk management practitioners, and AI engineers to develop and test tools and safeguard essential information systems, networks and workflows. It provides controlled testing environments to assess reliability, refine safeguards and develop practical approaches. The Lab openly shares tools, evidence, lessons and resources that partners can adapt and use in the field.

Across both areas, the aim is stronger local leadership and institutions. Where AI safety and effectiveness depend on them, our work can also strengthen field verification, professional skills, trusted relationships, governance and non-AI fallback arrangements.
